# Acne Marks, Scars, Pigmentation and Sensitive Skin: Why Your Skin May Not Be Improving

Acne disappears, but the dark marks remain.

You try to treat the marks, but another breakout appears.

You add another serum, acid or cleanser, and suddenly your skin starts burning, peeling or feeling unusually sensitive.

If this sounds familiar, the problem may not be that you have failed to find the “right product.”

The bigger problem may be that several different skin concerns are being treated as though they are the same thing.

Acne, post-acne pigmentation, true acne scars, tanning, melasma, oily skin and an irritated skin barrier can look related, but they do not necessarily have the same cause or require the same approach.

Understanding **what is actually happening to the skin** is therefore more useful than continuously adding new products.

---

## First: Two People With Similar-Looking Skin Can Have Different Problems

Consider two people who both say:

**“I have dark spots.”**

One may have pigmentation left after inflammatory acne.

Another may have melasma.

Someone else may have pigmentation following irritation, dermatitis, friction or another inflammatory condition.

Similarly, two people with acne may have very different patterns of disease.

Acne is considered a multifactorial inflammatory disorder involving the hair follicle and sebaceous gland. Sebum production, follicular blockage, inflammation, microorganisms and hormonal influences can all play roles.

This is one reason why a skincare routine that works extremely well for one person may not produce the same result for another.

A sensible starting question is therefore not simply:

**“Which product should I use?”**

It is:

**“What exactly am I trying to treat?”**

---

# 1. Why Does a Pimple Leave a Dark Mark?

For some people, the active pimple lasts only several days.

The mark it leaves behind can remain much longer.

This commonly happens because inflammation can stimulate changes in melanin production and distribution. The resulting discoloration is known as **post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation, or PIH**.

PIH can occur in any skin tone, but it may be more noticeable and persistent in darker skin types.

A 2024 systematic review examined studies involving **1,356 people with skin of colour**. Most PIH cases in the included data followed inflammatory conditions, and acne was the predominant inflammatory trigger among cases where the cause was reported.[1]

This helps explain an important pattern:

**Acne may disappear before the evidence of acne disappears.**

The skin can therefore appear uneven even after an individual pimple has healed.

## Treating the mark while ignoring new acne

This creates a common cycle.

A person develops acne.

The acne leaves pigmentation.

They begin treating the pigmentation.

More acne develops.

Those lesions create additional pigmentation.

The person then concludes that the pigmentation treatment is “not working.”

But part of the problem may be that the skin is continuing to develop new inflammation.

In many situations, preventing new inflammatory lesions is therefore just as important as trying to fade older marks.

---

# 2. Why More Brightening Products Do Not Always Mean Faster Results

When dark marks become frustrating, it is tempting to attack them from several directions.

A routine may gradually expand to include:

- vitamin C
- salicylic acid
- glycolic acid
- niacinamide
- azelaic acid
- kojic acid
- retinoids
- exfoliating toners
- scrubs
- pigmentation serums

Many of these ingredients have legitimate uses in dermatology and skincare.

The problem is not necessarily the individual ingredient.

The problem can be **how many products are being combined, how frequently they are being used, their strength, and whether the skin tolerates them**.

Irritation and inflammation can themselves contribute to pigmentation in susceptible skin.

A recent review of PIH in skin of colour emphasizes the importance of minimizing treatment-related inflammation and tailoring management to the individual because darker skin types can be more susceptible to pigmentary changes after irritation.[2]

This creates an unfortunate possibility:

**A person may try to treat pigmentation so aggressively that irritation contributes to additional pigmentation.**

The better question is therefore not:

**“How many brightening ingredients can I combine?”**

It is:

**“How can I treat the problem without creating another inflammatory problem?”**

---

# 3. Acne Marks and Acne Scars Are Not the Same Thing

This distinction is extremely important.

A **post-acne mark** primarily involves a change in colour.

A **true acne scar** involves a structural change in the skin.

For example, acne scarring may appear as:

- shallow depressions
- deeper pits
- rolling or uneven texture
- narrow deeper scars
- raised scars
- combinations of several scar patterns

A brown mark and a depressed acne scar may appear in the same area, but they are biologically different problems.

That means a product intended to improve pigmentation cannot necessarily correct a deep structural scar.

At the same time, someone whose main problem is pigmentation may not need an aggressive scar procedure.

This is why it is useful to determine whether the problem is primarily:

**colour, structure, active acne—or a combination of all three.**

---

# 4. Acne Scars Can Affect More Than Appearance

Acne scarring is sometimes dismissed as a purely cosmetic concern.

Research suggests that its effects can extend further.

A multinational study surveyed **723 adults with atrophic acne scars** and found meaningful effects on self-consciousness, social behaviour and relationships. For example, 27.5% reported embarrassment or self-consciousness related to their scars, while 35.5% reported avoiding public appearances.[3]

These statistics should not be interpreted to mean that every person with acne scars will experience psychological distress.

They do show that the emotional impact can be substantial for some people.

A few scars may look medically minor to an observer.

For the person seeing them every morning, their significance may be very different.

Skin concerns should therefore be discussed without exaggerating them—but also without dismissing the person's experience.

---

# 5. Why Controlling Active Acne Matters Before Focusing Only on Scars

When someone begins noticing pits or scars, attention naturally shifts toward removing them.

But continuing active acne can create additional scars.

Modern acne guidelines therefore focus on controlling the underlying acne rather than treating only its aftermath.

Evidence-based acne management may include different topical or systemic options depending on factors such as severity, pattern, previous response, scarring risk and individual circumstances.

A 2024 evidence-based acne guideline includes treatments such as benzoyl peroxide and topical retinoids among strongly recommended options, while other therapies receive different levels of recommendation depending on the evidence and clinical situation.[4]

This does **not** mean that everyone with acne should start these medications themselves.

It means something more important:

**Acne has established medical treatment approaches. Randomly changing products is not the same as following an appropriate treatment strategy.**

Persistent, painful or scarring acne deserves particular attention because preventing future scars can be easier than correcting established structural scars later.

---

# 6. “My Face Has Become Darker” Can Mean Several Different Things

Another common concern is:

**“My skin has become dark.”**

This statement should be interpreted carefully.

There is nothing unhealthy about naturally brown or darker skin.

What usually concerns people is a **change from their own normal skin tone**.

That change may reflect:

- tanning
- post-acne pigmentation
- melasma
- irritation-related pigmentation
- uneven sun exposure
- inflammation
- pigmentation around specific facial areas
- several processes occurring together

These conditions are not identical.

And the goal should not automatically be “fairer skin.”

A more appropriate goal is:

**healthy skin and improvement of abnormal or unwanted pigmentation while respecting the person's natural complexion.**

---

# 7. Sunlight Can Matter More Than People Realise

Sun exposure can make some pigmentary disorders harder to control.

Ultraviolet radiation has long been recognised as important, but research also shows that **visible light can contribute to pigmentation**, particularly in conditions such as melasma and in darker skin types.

A review of melasma and photoprotection describes contributions from ultraviolet radiation and visible light and emphasizes that photoprotection can be an important part of management.[5]

Another review examining sunscreen use in melasma and PIH found evidence that broad photoprotection can play a useful supporting role for pigmentary disorders in skin of colour.[6]

This helps explain why pigmentation sometimes seems to improve and then return.

A person may be concentrating entirely on removing pigment while continuing to experience the environmental exposure that contributes to it.

For persistent pigmentation, therefore, the question should not only be:

**“What can fade this?”**

It should also include:

**“What may be causing or worsening it?”**

---

# 8. Oily Skin Does Not Mean Dirty Skin

People with oily skin frequently describe the following experience:

They wash their face in the morning.

Within an hour or two, the forehead or entire face looks shiny again.

They begin washing more frequently.

Then they purchase a stronger cleanser.

The skin temporarily feels extremely clean.

Eventually it may start feeling tight, dry or irritated while still becoming oily later.

The assumption behind this behaviour is usually:

**“Oil is causing my acne, so I need to remove as much oil as possible.”**

But sebum is a normal part of skin physiology.

Acne involves more than the mere presence of surface oil. Follicular blockage, inflammatory activity, microorganisms, sebum-related processes and other biological factors interact in acne development.

The goal therefore does not need to be keeping the face completely oil-free throughout the day.

A more useful goal is controlling acne and excess oiliness without unnecessarily irritating the skin.

---

# 9. When Cleansing Becomes Too Aggressive

Cleansing is an important part of basic skin care.

But cleanser formulation and frequency matter.

Cleansers commonly rely on surfactants to help remove oils and other substances from the skin.

Scientific reviews of cleansing technology describe how surfactants can interact with proteins and lipids in the outer layer of the skin. Depending on the formulation and exposure, aggressive cleansing can contribute to dryness and impairment of barrier function.[7]

This helps explain why someone can experience what initially sounds contradictory:

**“My skin is oily but also dry.”**

Oil production and barrier condition are not exactly the same thing.

A person may continue producing significant sebum while simultaneously experiencing dryness, tightness or irritation.

That is why using an increasingly harsh cleanser is not always the answer to increasingly oily-feeling skin.

---

# 10. Sensitive or “Barrier-Damaged” Skin Often Starts With a Much Simpler Problem

A common sequence looks like this:

**Acne → acne treatment → pigmentation → pigmentation treatment → exfoliation → irritation → sensitivity → more products**

Eventually the person may say:

**“Everything burns my skin now.”**

Symptoms can include:

- burning
- stinging
- redness
- tightness
- peeling
- unusual dryness
- increased reactivity
- discomfort after products that were previously tolerated

The skin barrier is primarily associated with the outermost layers of the epidermis and plays an important role in limiting water loss and protecting against external irritants.

When skin is already irritated, introducing another strong active ingredient may simply introduce another source of irritation.

Sometimes a complicated routine needs **simplification**, not expansion.

This does not mean all active skincare ingredients are harmful.

It means that dose, frequency, formulation, combinations and individual tolerance matter.

---

# 11. “Natural” Does Not Automatically Mean Safer

When commercial skincare becomes frustrating, some people switch to home remedies.

A product may seem safe because it is natural, edible or commonly found in the kitchen.

But the skin does not determine irritation according to whether something is “natural” or “synthetic.”

Acidity, concentration, exposure time, allergens, irritant potential and the existing condition of the skin all matter.

This is why ingredients designed for food use should not automatically be assumed to be appropriate facial treatments.

The useful distinction is not:

**natural versus chemical**

but rather:

**appropriate versus inappropriate for the individual skin problem.**

---

# 12. Why Skin Problems Can Become Mentally Exhausting

Acne is visible.

That visibility can make it emotionally difficult.

People may repeatedly examine their skin in mirrors, worry about new lesions before an event, cover pigmentation, avoid photographs, change products constantly or feel that other people are noticing their skin.

A systematic review published in 2026 evaluated **101 studies** examining acne, quality of life and psychosocial outcomes. Despite considerable variation among the individual studies, the review found consistent evidence that acne can negatively affect quality of life and emotional or psychological well-being.[8]

This does not mean acne automatically causes anxiety, depression or other mental-health conditions.

It means skin disease can carry a genuine emotional burden.

That burden deserves acknowledgement rather than statements such as:

**“It's only acne.”**

At the same time, skincare should ideally reduce anxiety rather than encourage constant inspection or pursuit of impossible perfection.

---

# 13. Expensive Procedures Are Not Automatically Better Treatment

When creams and serums do not produce the expected result, people often start considering procedures such as:

- chemical peels
- microneedling
- fractional procedures
- different laser technologies
- scar procedures

Some of these treatments can be useful in appropriately selected patients.

But **expensive does not automatically mean appropriate**, and newer technology does not automatically mean better treatment for every skin type or every problem.

The 2024 systematic review of PIH treatment in skin of colour found improvement with several approaches but also highlighted limitations in the available evidence. Some laser-treated cases experienced worsening pigmentation.[1]

This is particularly important when treating skin that is prone to PIH.

A procedure can potentially improve one problem while also carrying a risk of inflammation, irritation or pigmentary change.

This does not mean procedures should be avoided.

It means the reason for performing the procedure should be clear.

Useful questions include:

**What exactly is being treated?**

**Is it pigmentation or a structural scar?**

**Is active acne controlled?**

**Why is this particular procedure being suggested?**

**What are the alternatives?**

**What degree of improvement is realistic?**

**How many sessions may be required?**

**What are the possible adverse effects for this skin type?**

A procedure should solve a defined problem, not simply be performed because a device is available.

---

# 14. Sometimes the Biggest Problem Is the Treatment Cycle

Consider a common scenario.

Someone develops acne.

They purchase an acne cleanser.

Then salicylic acid.

Then niacinamide.

Some acne improves, but dark marks remain.

They add a pigmentation serum.

Then an exfoliating acid.

The skin starts burning.

They add a “barrier repair” product.

Acne returns.

They change the sunscreen.

Then the moisturizer.

Then the cleanser.

Several months later, the original problem has become:

**acne + pigmentation + irritation + dryness + uncertainty**

There are now so many variables that the person cannot tell what is helping and what is making things worse.

This is when another product recommendation may provide less value than understanding the sequence of events.

Sometimes the most useful question becomes:

**“What can I stop?”**

rather than:

**“What else can I add?”**

---

# 15. Understand the Problem Before Increasing Treatment

A sensible framework for persistent skin concerns is:

**Identify the problem → consider likely causes → control continuing triggers → select appropriate treatment → allow enough time to evaluate it → reassess**

This is very different from:

**See a problem → buy multiple products → wait one week → change everything**

Skin treatment is biological.

Biological processes take time.

Different concerns also improve at different speeds.

An inflammatory pimple may settle relatively quickly.

The pigmentation that follows it may remain considerably longer.

A true scar is structurally different again.

Melasma may recur.

An irritated barrier may need a completely different approach from oily acne-prone skin.

A scientifically valid treatment can still be unsuitable for a particular person, concentration, frequency or combination.

That is why **understanding should come before intensity**.

---

# 16. Perfect Skin Should Not Be the Goal

Real human skin has:

- pores
- texture
- oil
- hair follicles
- lines
- occasional blemishes
- variations in colour

It changes with age, hormones, climate, inflammation, medication, sun exposure and numerous other factors.

The goal of evidence-based skin care should therefore not be impossible, permanently poreless, perfectly uniform skin.

A healthier goal may be:

**better-controlled acne**

**fewer new inflammatory lesions**

**reduced risk of additional scarring**

**appropriate improvement of unwanted pigmentation**

**less unnecessary irritation**

**better understanding of what the skin needs**

and, importantly,

**less anxiety about what to do next.**

---

# If You Feel Like You Have Tried Everything

If you are dealing with recurrent acne, dark acne marks, acne scars, pigmentation, tanning, oily clogged skin or sensitive reactive skin, another random product may not always be the missing answer.

Start by reconstructing the history of the problem.

Ask:

**What appeared first?**

**When did it begin?**

**Is acne still developing?**

**Does every breakout leave pigmentation?**

**Are the “marks” actually pigmentation, scars or both?**

**What products have already been tried?**

**Which products genuinely helped?**

**Which caused burning, peeling or irritation?**

**Were several new products introduced at the same time?**

**Is sun exposure contributing to pigmentation?**

**Are scars being treated while active acne is still continuing?**

**Is pigmentation being treated while the skin remains inflamed?**

And perhaps the most important question:

**Do I actually know what skin problem I am treating?**

Research on acne and pigmentation repeatedly points toward an important principle:

**These conditions are often influenced by multiple factors, so successful management usually depends on understanding the problem rather than continuously increasing the number or strength of products.**

Sometimes the best next step is not more skincare.

It is more clarity.

Because good skincare should eventually make your skin—and your life—easier to manage, not become another source of daily stress.

---

## When Professional Medical Assessment Is Particularly Important

General skincare information cannot diagnose an individual skin condition.

Medical assessment is particularly worth considering when acne is painful, severe, persistent or causing scars; when pigmentation is rapidly changing or difficult to identify; when the skin develops significant swelling, blistering, crusting or persistent burning; when a suspected treatment causes a strong reaction; or when a skin problem is having a substantial effect on emotional well-being.

Some conditions that resemble ordinary acne or pigmentation can have different causes and may need different treatment.
